Artist Statement

My recent art explores the women in my family and their often unseen labor in everyday life within the Hakka culture of Fujian, China. Though I grew up in Shanghai, I constantly visited my family in Fujian. There, I watched my aunts cook, clean, care for others, and hold entire communities together through acts that were rarely acknowledged. Many of the women I observed were expected to dedicate themselves entirely to serving fathers, husbands, and sons, often within environments that limited their education, independence, and sense of self. What inspires my work is not only the oppression they experienced, but the way they carried themselves through it with agency, care, and mutual support. Women shared advice during long afternoons washing clothes together, helped neighbors prepare meals for unexpected guests, and collectively raised children when others needed help. Their strength exists in their ability to continue building connection and structure within limitations. Through my artwork, I want to honor these women and recognize their invisible labor.

I explore these themes through mixed-media and printmaking processes using symbolism to convey messages. One of the symbols that reappears in my work is smoke, which symbolizes the way oppressive systems quietly invade everyday life. In one printmaking work, I portray a crowded household scene where men casually play cards as cigarette smoke drifts from their side of the room into a cramped kitchen packed with women preparing food. The men occupy space freely while the women, being overwhelmed and visually smothered by the smoke, remain confined to labor. In another piece, a man holds a cigarette whose burning cherry replaces where a woman’s head should be. Her body is gone, leaving only an empty apron behind. I applied bright acrylic color only to the glowing cigarette tip and the hollow apron while keeping the rest of the composition monochromatic. This contrast emphasizes how women are often reduced to roles and functions that erase individuality for the most trivial matters such as lighting a cigarette.

Although many of my themes engage with restriction and control, my creative process itself is rooted in freedom. Because the women before me were rarely allowed to live without limitation, I use artmaking as a way to experience and understand freedom for myself. Instead of strictly planning compositions beforehand, I work through an experimental, stream-of-consciousness approach that feels like a conversation between fragmented memories. I continuously reorganize images, rebuild connections, and reinterpret meaning through collaging original photographs from places I have lived, transferring images across surfaces, hand-drawing elements, cutting pieces apart, and repositioning them repeatedly before finalizing a composition. This process is especially evident in one work where a cruise ship, representing my newfound life in Florida, collides with a traditional Chinese wooden table. Although the table remains intact, the ceramic resting on top shatters, scattering across a fragmented yet connected landscape combining Fujian Hakka architecture, the Shanghai Bund, and Florida swamps. The piece reflects both rupture and continuity, showing how migration, memory, and identity can break apart while still remaining deeply connected.

Through my work, I invite viewers to reflect on inherited histories, personal transformation, and the ongoing process of rebuilding identity on one’s own terms.
